# Data Presser / DataHub: data-story research and content directions

Research discussion consolidated on 11 October 2026.

## Why we are doing this

Rufus’s starting concern was that the Data Presser story-development work did not appear to include a written collection or survey of existing data stories to learn from. Such research may already exist; the immediate task is to locate it or create it.

The purpose is to ground content development in concrete examples: what questions successful stories ask, how they build an argument, which data they use, and how their visualizations help. This collection should support subsequent brainstorming of stories for Data Presser and content for the DataHub blog.

The discussion established three content strands and a starting set of sources. A subsequent search supplied specific article and thread links. This document brings those together for hand-off to the project or another research session.

## Editorial brief

- Prioritize openly accessible material, including short pieces and blog-length articles; include selected longer essays.
- Look for clear, interesting arguments supported by data and effective visualizations.
- Prefer an editorial register closer to the Guardian, Financial Times and Economist.
- Favor approaches adaptable using existing datasets, without extensive original data collection.
- A public thread containing the central argument, charts and linked research counts as a usable example even when the associated article is paywalled.

These are Rufus’s editorial preferences, rather than a ranking of publishers’ overall quality. Our World in Data remains useful as a model of topical summaries and analytical essays, but is not the principal model for the journalism sought here. The Pudding represents a more playful, often original-data-intensive approach; retain it as a distinct category rather than the core model.

## Three content strands

### 1. Data stories

Articles that answer a concrete question or make an interesting argument with data. Use existing journalism as models for question selection, narrative structure, analysis and chart design.

### 2. Visual guides to reasoning and arguing with data

Accessible, visually engaging explanations of how to understand and make arguments with data. Initial ideas include line charts, regression analysis and how to construct a well-supported argument. The aspiration is a Tufte-inspired combination of intellectual substance, thoughtful visual presentation and enjoyment.

### 3. Key figures, approaches and works

Profiles and reviews introducing influential practitioners of data analysis, visualization and storytelling. A concrete starting idea is an introduction to Edward Tufte’s approach and a review of his work for the DataHub blog. *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information* was proposed as a possible starting book; the review’s scope remains undecided. It was not the subject of the article-link research below.

## Specific examples with source links

The following sources were retrieved in the follow-up research. The assessments of their usefulness are editorial judgments. Retrieval confirmed available text; it did not test every chart, historical interactive or linked dataset. Thread summaries report the author’s argument, rather than independently validating it.

### Data stories: closest matches

#### 1. John Burn-Murdoch — Britain’s dependence on London

- **Source:** public Twitter thread reproduced by Thread Reader.
- **Link:** https://threadreaderapp.com/thread/1689950429403037697.html
- **Format:** 12 posts; approximately four minutes according to Thread Reader.
- **Argument:** national averages conceal regional differences; Britain’s economic output is unusually concentrated in London.
- **Approach:** compare regions internationally, then examine how removing the dominant region changes national GDP per capita.
- **Why useful:** an example of turning a familiar national comparison into a more revealing argument through disaggregation.
- **Methodological point:** the author distinguishes GDP per capita from household incomes and living standards.

#### 2. John Burn-Murdoch — The ideological divide between young men and women

- **Source:** public Twitter thread reproduced by Thread Reader, 26 January 2024.
- **Link:** https://threadreaderapp.com/thread/1750849189834022932.html
- **Format:** 31 posts; approximately eight minutes according to Thread Reader.
- **Argument:** treating young people as one political generation conceals differences between young men and women.
- **Approach:** compare survey results across countries, age groups and sexes.
- **Why useful:** challenges an aggregate narrative by separating the underlying groups.
- **Adaptation point:** distinguish observed survey patterns from proposed explanations for them.

#### 3. John Burn-Murdoch — Evidence on rent control

- **Source:** public Bluesky thread, 4 October 2026.
- **Link:** https://bsky.app/profile/jburnmurdoch.ft.com/post/3mx2kxpixo22x
- **Format:** four posts, including a chart and research link.
- **Argument:** rent control benefits covered tenants but can have adverse effects elsewhere in the housing market.
- **Approach:** visualize findings from a review of 112 studies.
- **Underlying research:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1051137724000020
- **Why useful:** a compact model for translating published research into a chart-led argument.
- **Access:** the public thread provides the argument without requiring the associated FT article.

#### 4. Mona Chalabi / Guardian — “Families spend £489 each week - on what?”

- **Date:** 11 December 2013.
- **Link:** https://www.theguardian.com/news/datablog/2013/dec/11/families-spend-489-each-week-on-what
- **Focus:** household expenditure.
- **Why useful:** official statistics become an accessible story about everyday life.

#### 5. Mona Chalabi / Guardian — “UK median weekly pay is £517 - but who earns that?”

- **Date:** 12 December 2013.
- **Link:** https://www.theguardian.com/news/datablog/2013/dec/12/uk-median-weekly-pay-is-517-but-who-earns-that
- **Focus:** earnings.
- **Why useful:** a compact model for unpacking a headline statistic.

#### 6. Guardian — “Egg prices have quadrupled, chicken has doubled: the cost of US inflation in 10 items”

- **Date:** 22 March 2025.
- **Link:** https://www.theguardian.com/business/datablog/2025/mar/22/egg-prices-us-inflation-cost
- **Why useful:** familiar purchases make inflation concrete.

### Longer visual journalism

#### 7. Financial Times — Coronavirus tracker

- **Link:** https://www.ft.com/content/a2901ce8-5eb7-4633-b89c-cbdf5b386938
- **Format:** longer visual narrative, labelled “Free to read” in the retrieved source.
- **Content:** pandemic charts, international comparisons and excess mortality.
- **Why useful:** a reference for assembling related charts into a coherent narrative.
- **Fit:** more extensive than the short pieces primarily sought here.

### Visual explanation and reasoning with data

#### 8. Seeing Theory — Regression Analysis

- **Source:** Seeing Theory, hosted by Brown University.
- **Link:** https://seeing-theory.brown.edu/regression-analysis/
- **Format:** interactive visual explanation.
- **Content:** ordinary least squares, movable data points and Anscombe’s quartet.
- **Why useful:** directly relevant to the regression-guide idea: readers can manipulate observations and see the fitted line change.
- **Status:** the site is marked as archived for reference.

#### 9. Mike Bostock — “For Example”

- **Date:** 9 June 2013.
- **Link:** https://bost.ocks.org/mike/example/
- **Format:** illustrated talk transcript.
- **Content:** how examples communicate ideas, with detailed demonstrations involving map projections and adaptive resampling.
- **Why useful:** a model for explaining technical ideas visually while making their purpose clear.
- **Correction:** the earlier conversation characterized it as an account of Times graphics. The retrieved essay is primarily about examples and visual explanation, rather than a survey of New York Times graphics.

## Brief for the next research session

Build on the linked examples above. Produce an assessed collection of openly accessible data journalism, emphasizing short and blog-length pieces in the Guardian / FT editorial register. Public threads with substantive charts and arguments are acceptable. For each example, identify the question, claim, source data, analytical method, visualization choices and feasibility of adaptation using existing open data. Keep journalistic stories distinct from topical summaries, interactive teaching and profiles of practitioners. Then propose specific Data Presser / DataHub stories grounded in that collection.
