# Outline: Where France’s public money goes

The prose renders this argument; if it changes the argument, revise and re-review the outline first. Story, outline, chart script and SVGs live in `site/stories/`. The underlying [dataset](https://github.com/datasets/datapressr/tree/main/datasets/france-public-finances) has passed independent adversarial review. Bead `datapressr-sy2` tracks the work. The longer [investigation note](https://github.com/datasets/datapressr/blob/main/docs/france-public-finances-investigation.md) records source reconciliation and limits.

## Argument in one sentence

France has spent more than it raised in every year of the available 1995–2025 record, with social protection and health now accounting for most spending; understanding the persistent gap requires examining receipts and spending commitments together.

## Scope

An English explainer for a general reader, motivated by current debt-market reporting. Establish the debt stock, recent deficits, spending mix and the accounting and attributed mechanisms behind the gap. Cover general government, including central, local and social-security administrations. Do not equate this with the central state budget. The dataset shows accounting outcomes, not which programmes ought to be cut or what an optimal tax rate is. No claim to have apportioned decades of debt causally between policy decisions, demographics and shocks. No forecast or bond yield is presented as an observed fiscal outturn.

## Argument in order

1. **Lead with the gap.** Chart 1 shows revenue and expenditure from 1995 through 2025, as percentages of GDP. All 31 observed French B9 values are negative. In 2025 expenditure was €1,714.1 billion (57.2% of GDP), revenue €1,561.6 billion (52.1%), leaving €152.5 billion (5.1%). These are the current Eurostat snapshot values, rounded from millions of euros only for display. Revenue includes taxes, social contributions, sales and other receipts. Do not compare all spending with taxes alone. Do not describe 31 years as an independently established fifty-year record.
2. **Where it goes.** Chart 2 uses the latest available functional breakdown, 2024. The ten divisions sum, within source rounding, to €1,671.8 billion. Social protection is €693.0 billion / 41.5% of spending; health €261.2 billion / 15.6%. Together 57.1%. Education is €148.6 billion / 8.9%; defence €54.2 billion / 3.2%. All ten divisions remain visible so small categories are not hidden. Old age is €392.0 billion, inside social protection, not an additional slice. COFOG social protection excludes health; do not call all of it pensions. General public services includes public debt transactions; interest is not an eleventh COFOG division. Salaries and investment are economic classifications overlapping these purposes.
3. **Why a large gap can persist.** The descriptive composition does not by itself establish the cause. From 2022 to 2024, spending declined from 58.4% to 57.0% of GDP (−1.4 points), but revenue declined further, from 53.7% to 51.2% (−2.5 points). The deficit rose from 4.7% to 5.8%. Chart 1 annotates these endpoints and arithmetic. INSEE attributes 2023 receipt weakness chiefly to slower taxable bases, including corporate profits and property transactions, with tax cuts also contributing. INSEE attributes much of 2024 pension spending growth to indexation after earlier inflation. Neither mechanism is estimated from the CSVs themselves. For the longer-running element, the IMF estimates a 2025 structural deficit of 5.0% of potential GDP and a primary deficit of 3.0% of GDP. These are attributed external estimates, not extra observed quantities; they support the limited point that neither the business cycle nor interest alone explains the gap. The primary balance excludes interest; structural balance adjusts for the cycle and one-off factors using an estimated potential output.
4. **Give the counter-evidence.** Chart 3 shows annual deficits 2019–2025: 2.4, 8.9, 6.6, 4.7, 5.4, 5.8 and 5.1% of GDP. The 2025 gap narrowed. INSEE describes new revenue measures and slower spending growth as contributing. Social protection is the biggest category, yet its GDP share fell from 24.5% in 2014 to 23.7% in 2024; chart 2 carries this caveat. A rising euro cost is not necessarily a rising share of the economy, and a big category is not proof of waste. The 2025 revenue rebound and slower spending do not erase the remaining deficit.
5. **Connect the flow to the stock.** Chart 4 shows quarterly debt/GDP from 2000-Q1 to 2026-Q2, all from one INSEE release. Mark 2007-Q4 65.5%, 2019-Q4 98.2%, 2020-Q4 114.9%, 2023-Q4 109.5%, and 2026-Q2 119.0%. Latest gross debt is €3,595.5 billion. The ratio fell after the pandemic before rising again. Repeated deficits contribute to the stock, but debt changes also include financial transactions and other adjustments; debt/GDP also changes with nominal GDP. A deficit is an annual flow; debt is a quarter-end stock. These figures do not establish imminent default or explain the current bond yield.
6. **Method last.** Short links to dataset, offline build and chart script. Data are Eurostat and INSEE, saved on 8 October 2026. Different calendars: 2025 annual accounts; 2024 functional spending; June 2026 debt. Preserve revisions and labels. The source methodology and reconciliation live in the dataset README; prose includes the brief vintage caveat. Author's voice pass remains outstanding.

## Outside context and sources

- [INSEE, 2023 public accounts, 31 May 2024](https://www.insee.fr/fr/statistiques/8194620): explanation of taxable bases and tax changes; use for the attributed mechanism, not its superseded headline deficit number.
- [INSEE, spending by function in 2024, 5 February 2026](https://www.insee.fr/fr/statistiques/8735252): pension indexation, health and the withdrawal of energy support; source of interpretation, not a causal estimate derived here.
- [INSEE, 2025 public accounts, 29 May 2026](https://www.insee.fr/fr/statistiques/8997691): new revenue measures, benefit indexation and slower expenditure growth. This article's 57.3/52.2% spending/revenue ratios differ from the archived Eurostat snapshot's 57.2/52.1%; both give a 5.1% deficit. Do not mix vintages within a chart.
- [IMF, July 2026 Article IV assessment](https://www.imf.org/en/news/articles/2026/07/22/pr26255-france-imf-executive-board-concludes-2026-article-iv-consultation): 2025 primary and structural balances, model-based interpretation and ageing pressures. Policy recommendations remain the IMF's judgements. The story need not endorse them.
- [INSEE, Q2 2026 debt, 29 September 2026](https://www.insee.fr/fr/statistiques/9053525): debt concept and revised history. The current release gives Q2 2025 as 115.2%; the motivating Euronews article's 115.6% comparison is not substituted into this chart.

All chart numbers and annotations read the CSV rows at build time. `france-public-finances-data.mjs` reads RFC 4180 CSV using d3-dsv and reproduces the core outline arithmetic; `france-public-finances-make-charts.mjs` will implement this plan with Observable Plot static SVGs. ## Voice and friction notes

Use the [house voice](../docs/voice-guide.md): concrete, attributed, British spelling; 300–700 words of prose. The user's “what and why” brief needs four charts rather than the usual one or two. Each has a distinct purpose; avoid turning the prose into a tour of columns. The fuller research note holds detail that would overwhelm the story.

Exempt from the “every data number on a chart” rule: the dataset-wide count of31 observed deficit years; the explicitly attributed IMF primary/structural estimates (3.0% GDP and 5.0% potential GDP); publication/retrieval dates and methodological vintage numbers, if mentioned. All other prose data numbers must appear as chart labels or annotations. Charts do not constitute a full causal decomposition; neither spending composition nor the structural-balance estimate assigns blame to particular policies.
