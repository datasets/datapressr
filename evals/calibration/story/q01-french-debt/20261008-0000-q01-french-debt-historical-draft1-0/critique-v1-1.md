## Strongest findings missed

- The story never establishes whether France faces acute financing stress or a longer-term fiscal problem; dated borrowing-cost, refinancing and investor-demand evidence would distinguish them.
- The 2025 deficit remained 2.7 percentage points of GDP above its 2019 level despite the latest improvement, a useful benchmark visible in the supplied chart.
- The debt ratio rose 9.5 percentage points between end-2023 and Q2 2026, reversing the preceding decline, but the story does not explain the reversal.
- Interest expenditure and comparable-country figures are missing, leaving readers unable to judge how exceptional or burdensome France’s position is.
- The story omits how political constraints and euro-area rules or support affect France’s capacity to adjust and finance its debt.
- There is no explanation of the balance between growth, interest rates and the primary budget needed to stabilise debt, or of who would bear the costs of adjustment.

## Lessons

1. Answer the commissioned question in the lead, and make descriptive background serve that answer. Evidence: The commission asks why there is a debt crisis; the lead establishes a persistent spending–revenue gap without establishing a crisis.
2. Distinguish the size of a spending category from its contribution to fiscal deterioration. Evidence: The story correctly notes that social protection’s GDP share fell despite its being the largest spending category.
3. Pair debt-stock figures with evidence about financing conditions when discussing a debt crisis. Evidence: The 119.0% debt ratio is well labelled, but borrowing costs, refinancing exposure and investor confidence are absent.
4. Use improvements as a test of the argument and compare them with a meaningful baseline. Evidence: The deficit chart shows the 2025 improvement and the much smaller 2019 deficit, but the prose develops only the first comparison.
5. Explain adjustment through both its economic mechanism and its distributional consequences. Evidence: The primary deficit is defined, but readers learn neither what would stabilise debt nor who would pay for the necessary changes.
