## Strongest findings missed

- The story supplies no dated evidence of financing stress or a loss of policy credibility, so it never establishes whether “crisis” is the right description.
- The supplied figures show the deficit widening by 1.1 percentage points of GDP between 2022 and 2024 because revenue fell by 2.5 points while spending fell by 1.4 points; this is the clearest quantified explanation and deserves the lead.
- The debt chart shows a 9.5-percentage-point rise between end-2023 and June 2026, a recent reversal that the prose leaves unquantified and unexplained.
- The interest bill, its change over time and comparable countries’ fiscal positions are missing, preventing the reader from judging how exceptional or costly France’s position is.
- The story omits the political and euro-area constraints on adjustment and the distribution of costs among taxpayers, benefit recipients and public-service users.
- No debt-stabilisation calculation connects the primary balance, borrowing costs and nominal growth, leaving the reader without a benchmark for the adjustment required.

## The one change that matters most

Rebuild the story around whether and why France faces a debt crisis now, leading with dated evidence of fiscal or financing pressure and explaining why political constraints, growth and borrowing costs make stabilisation difficult; compress the spending inventory to supporting context.

## Lessons

1. Answer the commissioned question in the opening claim, and use descriptive breakdowns only where they advance that answer. Evidence: The commission asks why there is a debt crisis; the title and opening instead ask where public money goes.
2. Distinguish a large debt stock from a current crisis using dated evidence of financing conditions or policy constraints. Evidence: The story reports debt of 119% of GDP without showing investor concern, a policy trigger or an inability to finance spending.
3. Separate spending levels from explanations of changes in the deficit. Evidence: Social protection dominates the budget, but the 2022–2024 deficit deterioration occurred while spending fell relative to GDP and revenue fell further.
4. Preserve counter-evidence and distinguish nominal amounts from economic burdens. Evidence: The story correctly retains the 2025 deficit improvement and explains why a 31% nominal increase in social protection can accompany a declining GDP share.
5. Give each chart one principal finding and reserve detailed inventories for supporting material. Evidence: The evolution figure combines aggregate histories, nine subgroup comparisons and nominal-growth accounting in one visual block.
