## Strongest findings missed

- The debt chart shows a 16.7-percentage-point increase in 2020, a 5.4-point decline through 2023 and a 9.5-point rebound by June 2026, a useful chronology that the story never explains.
- The 2025 deficit remained 2.7 percentage points of GDP above its 2019 level despite its latest improvement, sharpening the question of why the pandemic-era deterioration has not fully unwound.
- The reported total and pre-interest deficits suggest interest payments of roughly 2.1% of GDP in 2025, but the Eurostat and IMF definitions and vintages would need reconciliation before publishing that calculation.
- There is no evidence of market stress or a dated policy trigger—such as borrowing spreads, refinancing pressure or a documented loss of fiscal credibility—to establish why this is a crisis now.
- Comparable-country debt and interest burdens, debt-stabilisation scenarios and the euro’s institutional role are missing, leaving the reader unable to judge whether France’s debt is manageable.
- The spending breakdown identifies beneficiaries but supplies no proposed adjustment, political constraints or distributional evidence showing who would bear its costs.

## Lessons

1. Keep the commissioned question as the organising argument, even when the available data supports an appealing adjacent story. Evidence: The commission asks why France has a debt crisis; the title and opening instead ask where public money goes.
2. Distinguish evidence of a large debt burden from evidence of an immediate crisis. Evidence: The story establishes persistent deficits and debt of 119% of GDP but presents no lender behaviour, refinancing pressure or political trigger.
3. Use spending composition to explain budget size, and separate evidence to explain changes in deficits and debt. Evidence: The story correctly observes that social protection’s large share cannot explain a deficit widening while spending falls relative to GDP.
4. Give each chart one main comparison and place the decisive evidence in the plotted area. Evidence: The evolution figure combines aggregate history, subgroup changes and nominal growth, while the revenue chart relegates its key recent comparison to a footnote.
5. Explain debt sustainability through mechanisms and feasible choices, including who bears their costs. Evidence: The final paragraph distinguishes stocks from flows but stops before borrowing costs, euro membership, stabilisation options or distributional consequences.
