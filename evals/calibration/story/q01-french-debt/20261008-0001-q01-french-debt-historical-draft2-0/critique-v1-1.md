## Strongest findings missed

- The supplied figures show that the 2022–2024 deficit deterioration of 1.1 percentage points of GDP reflects a 2.5-point revenue decline partly offset by a 1.4-point spending decline, a stronger central finding than the spending ranking.
- The debt ratio rose 9.5 percentage points between end-2023 and June 2026 despite the annual deficit narrowing in 2025, which deserves an explanation of why improvement need not mean stabilisation.
- The 5.1% overall deficit and IMF estimate of a 3% primary deficit suggest an interest bill around 2.1% of GDP in 2025, but their definitions and vintages need reconciliation before presenting that estimate.
- There is no dated evidence of market stress or a policy trigger that would establish why this is a crisis now.
- Comparable-country benchmarks, political and euro-area constraints, and the distributional consequences of proposed remedies are absent.
- A debt-stabilisation calculation using explicit growth and effective interest-rate assumptions would show the scale of adjustment required and the risks to it.

## Lessons

1. Make the central argument answer the commissioned question before choosing supporting detail. Evidence: The commission asks why there is a debt crisis; the headline and opening instead ask where public money goes.
2. Distinguish evidence of a large debt stock from evidence of an immediate crisis. Evidence: The story establishes debt of 119% of GDP but supplies no market-stress evidence or dated policy trigger.
3. Use changes in fiscal components to explain deterioration; spending rankings alone cannot establish its cause. Evidence: The revenue decline between 2022 and 2024 explains the widening gap more directly than the three spending-composition charts.
4. Connect a falling deficit to the conditions required for a stable debt ratio. Evidence: The story reports an improving 2025 deficit and renewed debt growth without explaining how both can occur.
5. Preserve distinctions between nominal growth, GDP shares and service volumes. Evidence: The story clearly explains why social protection can cost 31% more euros while taking a smaller share of GDP, without claiming that services increased proportionately.
