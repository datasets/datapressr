# Independent reader session

Before reading: I have not previously seen either packet or its story in this session. I have general knowledge of public datasets, economics, and reading data journalism, but do not yet know these stories' topic. I have no known exposure to their author, treatment assignment, expected answers, or evaluation notes. Reading order is A, then B, as assigned.

## A — spontaneous observations, saved before B

Topic familiarity after opening: I have general prior knowledge of temperature anomalies, climate warming and annual record comparisons, but no prior knowledge of these particular 2026 figures or this story.

Main takeaway: 2026 has accumulated enough of a deficit to 2024 that even repeating the hottest recorded final quarter would not make it the warmest year. That does not make it a cool year, and this is arithmetic about a hypothetical finish rather than a prediction.

What changed my understanding: The scale mismatch between nine months already counted and only three left made the result feel intuitive. The gap at year end can look small—less than 0.05°C—while the required final quarter has to clear the previous hottest quarter by 0.17–0.18°C. Those are different comparisons, and putting them together was the useful insight. The historical-dot chart also gave me a much stronger visual sense of how far above earlier years the recent final quarters sit.

Weakest passage: The opening definition paragraph slows the story with a list of things the measure is not. I understand why those distinctions are there, but I wanted to get to the numerical problem sooner. The repeated cautions about averaging choices and rounding also make the flow feel a little defensive, particularly 'reaching the rounded label does not settle the comparison.'

## B — spontaneous observations

Main takeaway: To beat 2024, the last quarter of 2026 needs to be warmer, relative to its seasonal baseline, than any recorded last quarter—roughly 1.02–1.03°C above baseline just to tie, beyond the prior 0.85°C high. The old record is not a physical limit, so this does not tell us the chance of that happening.

What changed my understanding: Having already read A, B did not add a new factual understanding for me. It changed the emphasis: the historical hurdle is the opening event, and the cumulative-average explanation becomes the supporting reason. The opening scatterplot made the unusually high finish required visually immediate. I liked the explicit sentence that a high anomaly need not mean the closing months are the hottest in absolute temperature.

Weakest passage: The second prose paragraph arrives very early with weighting, physical uncertainty and unrounded thresholds, before I have the simple explanation for why the finish has to be so high. 'Supplies the timely question' also sounds like editorial scaffolding instead of telling me the question directly. The story takes several paragraphs after its big chart and number to explain the nine-month deficit.

Actual viewing and limits: I read all of B/index.html as source text, including its alt text and hidden captions, then viewed B/desktop.png and B/mobile.png through the image tool at default high detail. As for A, these are full-page scaled previews of screenshots with native viewport widths 1280 and 390; I did not inspect them in a browser at native size. On the desktop preview I could read prose and the principal chart titles, values and annotations, with very small credits less comfortable. On mobile the prose remained readable, the opening dot plot and threshold were visually legible as shapes, but the chart's fine text was tiny. The opening chart therefore asks more of a mobile reader before the prose begins. The second chart likewise needed the prose/source text to supply comfortable access to exact values. I did not enlarge any charts in either packet.

## Preference and possible influences

I prefer A. Its headline gives me the concrete surprising result, and its opening paragraph explains both the scenario and the nine-month reason before asking me to parse technical distinctions. Moving from 'even the hottest known finish falls short' to 'what would it actually take?' feels like a natural question I would ask as a reader. B has the stronger immediate visual, especially on desktop, but the early caveats interrupt my path from its striking hurdle number to an explanation of that number. On mobile, A also gives me a usable takeaway before I encounter a tiny chart; B puts the tiny chart immediately below a less informative headline.

The preference is not evidence that the chart-first structure cannot work. I read A first, so A got the first opportunity to teach me the argument and B inevitably felt partly repetitive. That could influence my preference. Conversely, having learned the definitions from A probably made B's opening chart easier to understand than it would have been cold.

I recognized no author identity or hidden treatment assignment. I noticed the same chart names, source credit naming DataHub, numbers, style and reproduction instructions in both packets, and a deliberate-looking difference in sequence and headline. Those are visible packet clues only; I did not consult an author mapping or infer which was the intended winner.
