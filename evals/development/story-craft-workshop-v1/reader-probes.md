## A

1. Repeating October–December 2023 would bring 2026 to an annual anomaly of about 0.67–0.68°C, close to but below 2024's 0.72°C. The first nine months of 2026 averaged 0.62°C versus 2024's 0.70–0.71°C for the same period. Three months matching the hottest previous final quarter cannot compensate for that nine-month deficit.

2. The degrees are global surface air temperature anomalies across land and ocean relative to 1991–2020, with each month compared to its own monthly baseline. They are not absolute temperatures, local weather or a preindustrial warming measure. The ranges show differences between equal-month and day-weighted averaging, not physical uncertainty. The 0.85°C figure is the previous hottest final-quarter anomaly; 1.02–1.03°C is the final-quarter anomaly needed to tie 2024; 0.17–0.18°C is how far that hurdle exceeds the previous final-quarter maximum. Annual and January–September numbers summarize their respective periods.

3. It establishes a conditional arithmetic requirement, not a probability or forecast. Beating 2024 would require exceeding the unrounded tie hurdle and therefore an unprecedented final-quarter anomaly in this series. The historical maximum is not a ceiling. The calculation does not evaluate the reported El Niño outlook or establish how likely the needed finish is.

4. Less than 0.05°C annually: 2026 would finish at 0.67–0.68°C versus 2024 at 0.72°C. That is different from the larger 0.17–0.18°C increment over the previous final-quarter maximum required to reach the tie hurdle.

## B

1. Matching late 2023 would still leave 2026 below 2024 for the year, at approximately 0.67–0.68°C compared with 0.72°C. The annual average includes the already cooler January–September period, so the closing quarter must offset the accumulated deficit, not merely match the hottest earlier closing quarter. A repeat of 2023's 0.85°C final quarter is insufficient.

2. They measure anomalies in global surface air temperature relative to the corresponding months' 1991–2020 averages, and averages or differences of those anomalies. The seasonal reference matters: a very high closing-quarter anomaly does not mean those months have the highest absolute temperatures. Ranges compare weighting every month equally with weighting by its day count; they are not physical uncertainty intervals. The headline hurdle of 1.02–1.03°C is the closing-quarter average needed for a tie, and 0.17–0.18°C measures its excess over the previous maximum of 0.85°C. The annual comparison and first-nine-month comparison refer to different averaging periods.

3. It does not assign a chance to a new record. It shows how high the final quarter would need to be and compares that requirement with history. An unprecedented outcome is not ruled out by being unprecedented. Reported strengthening El Niño conditions are mentioned but not tested by this calculation, and exceeding a rounded displayed threshold is not enough to settle the exact record comparison.

4. The remaining annual difference is less than 0.05°C in the repeat scenario: 0.67–0.68°C for 2026 against 0.72°C for 2024.
